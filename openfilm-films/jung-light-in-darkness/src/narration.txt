At the end of his life, Carl Jung said that his story was not the story of what happened to him. It was the story of what rose up from inside him.
He compared a human life to a plant that lives on its rhizome. The real life is hidden underground, in the root. What shows above the soil is only a flower, and the flower lasts a single summer.
So when he finally told his story, in his eighties, he barely mentioned the outer events: the jobs, the honours, the travels. He said they had faded, that they felt hollow next to the other thing.
What he remembered with total clarity were his dreams, his visions, and his long struggle with the unconscious. Those, he said, were the raw material of everything he ever did.
This is that other story. The story underneath.
# The Thing Under the Ground
> "The dream is a little hidden door in the innermost and most secret recesses of the soul." Carl Jung, The Meaning of Psychology for Modern Man.
It begins in a parsonage in Switzerland, with a little boy, barely three or four years old, and a dream he would never forget.
In the dream, he is in a meadow near his home, and he finds a hole in the ground, lined with stone. A stairway leads down into the dark.
Afraid, but curious, he goes down. At the bottom there is an arched doorway, closed by a heavy green curtain. He pushes it aside.
Behind it is a long stone chamber, and at the far end, on a golden throne, stands something enormous. At first he thinks it is a tree trunk.
But it is made of skin and flesh. At its top is a round head with no face and no hair, and a single eye, staring upward without moving.
The boy is frozen with terror. Then, from somewhere above, he hears his mother's voice: "Look at him. That is the man-eater!" He wakes up drenched in sweat.
Decades later, Jung understood that the thing on the throne was an ancient ritual symbol, an underground god, a god of the earth and of the body, the opposite of the gentle Jesus his father preached about every Sunday.
Every time the adults spoke too sweetly about Lord Jesus, the boy would think of the other one, the one under the ground.
He later said that this dream was his initiation. Before he knew anything about light, he had been shown the darkness.
There was another secret. In the garden, on a slope, there was a stone that stuck out of the earth. He called it his stone.
He would sit on it for hours and play a strange game with himself. Am I the one sitting on the stone? Or am I the stone, and he is sitting on me?
As he grew, the boy noticed that he seemed to be two people.
The first was the son of his parents: a schoolboy, awkward, often lazy, not as clever or as clean as the other boys. Jung later called him personality number one.
The second was old. Sceptical, solitary, distant from people but close to nature, to the night, to dreams, and to God. Personality number two.
For years he thought something was wrong with him. Only much later did he realise that this was not a split or a sickness. It is the play of opposites that lives inside every one of us.
# The Little Light
> "The sole purpose of human existence is to kindle a light in the darkness of mere being." Carl Jung, Memories, Dreams, Reflections.
As school ended, Jung was torn. He loved nature and science. He also loved history, philosophy, the ancient worlds of Egypt and Babylon. He dreamed of becoming an archaeologist, but his family had no money.
Then came a dream. Deep in a forest he found a hidden pool, and lying in the clear water was a wonderful creature, round, shimmering with every colour, made of countless tiny cells.
He woke with his heart pounding, burning to know. He would study the natural sciences. He would study medicine.
And yet, even then, he hadn't found his path. At the very end of his studies, he opened the last textbook on his list, the one on psychiatry. He expected nothing from it.
In the preface, the author called mental illnesses "diseases of the personality". Jung's heart began to pound so hard that he had to stand up.
In a flash he saw that here, and only here, nature and spirit met. His two personalities could finally flow into one river.
Around then, he had a dream. It was night, and he was walking against a violent wind, through thick fog, holding a tiny flame cupped in his hands. It could go out at any moment.
He looked back, and saw a gigantic black figure following him.
And yet, in the middle of his terror, he knew one thing: whatever happened, he had to keep that little light alive.
When he woke, he understood. The giant was only his own shadow, cast by the very light he was carrying. And the light was his consciousness: tiny and fragile against the dark, but the only light he had.
He took a post at the Burghölzli, a large asylum in Zurich. In those days, psychiatry mostly sorted patients into categories and locked them away. The person behind the illness hardly mattered.
Jung began to do something unusual. He listened.
He found that even the wildest delusions had meaning. Behind every breakdown there was a story, a hidden life, a secret, and if you could find that secret, you held the key to the cure.
One patient was a young woman who had stopped speaking entirely. Over many weeks, very slowly, Jung earned her trust, and finally she told him: she had been living on the moon.
By telling Jung her story, she broke the spell of that other world. She came back down to earth. She recovered, and later she even married.
From that moment, Jung never saw the mentally ill the same way. Their inner worlds were not noise. They were rich, real, and full of meaning.
He came to believe that a doctor cannot heal from behind a desk. He has to meet the patient face to face, one human being to another, and he has to be touched by the patient's suffering himself.
As he would later put it, "Only the wounded physician heals."
# The Descent
> "One does not become enlightened by imagining figures of light, but by making the darkness conscious." Carl Jung, The Philosophical Tree.
For a few years, Jung was the favourite son of Sigmund Freud. Freud even called him his crown prince.
But Jung could not accept that everything in the psyche came down to sex. In nineteen twelve he published his own ideas, and the friendship broke apart. He lost his mentor and most of his colleagues. Suddenly he was standing on nothing.
He felt a terrible pressure building inside him, so strong that he wondered if he was losing his mind.
So he made a strange decision. Since he knew nothing at all, he would simply do whatever came to him. He would surrender to the unconscious.
The first thing that came up was a memory: himself, as a boy of ten or eleven, playing for hours with building blocks, making little houses and castles.
And the memory came with a wave of emotion. That little boy still had something alive in him, something creative that the grown man had lost.
He gathered stones from the lake shore, and every day, after lunch and before his patients arrived, he played. He built cottages, a castle, a whole village.
Then, in October of nineteen thirteen, on a train journey, a vision hit him.
He saw a monstrous flood covering all of Europe, from the North Sea to the Alps. He saw the rubble of civilisation floating in yellow waves, and the drowned bodies of countless thousands. Then the whole sea turned to blood.
He was sure he was going mad. Then, on the first of August, nineteen fourteen, the First World War broke out.
Now his task was clear. He had to understand what had happened, and how his own inner experience was tied to the fate of the whole world.
For years he let the fantasies come. He wrote them down in black notebooks, and later copied and painted them, by hand, into a great red leather book.
He learned something that saved his life: as long as he could turn the raw emotions into images, he was calm. If he had left the images buried in the emotions, they would have torn him apart.
In these depths he met figures who seemed to have a life of their own. The most important was an old man with the horns of a bull and the wings of a kingfisher. He called him Philemon.
Philemon told him something that changed everything. Thoughts are not things you make. They are like animals in a forest, or people in a room, or birds in the air. You do not create them. You meet them.
But Jung knew how dangerous this road was. He had seen what happened to Nietzsche, who lived only in the world of his thoughts until they swallowed him.
So he held on to the earth. To his wife and children. To his patients. To ordinary daily life. Every night he went down into the dark, and every morning he came back up.
And slowly, a new shape began to appear. Every morning he sketched a small circle in a notebook: a mandala. And each one showed him, exactly, the state of his soul that day.
In time, he saw that all of his paths, all of his wandering, were circling around a single point. The centre. What he would call the Self.
# Stone and Water
> "In all chaos there is a cosmos, in all disorder a secret order." Carl Jung, The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious.
For the next twenty years, Jung searched for proof that what he had gone through was not just personal madness. Had anyone walked this road before?
He found the answer in the most unexpected place: in old, dusty books of alchemy.
On the surface, the alchemists were trying to turn lead into gold. But Jung saw that their strange pictures, of kings and queens, dragons and suns, death and rebirth in the sealed vessel, were really maps of the soul.
The alchemists had been projecting their inner transformation into matter. Their work was his work. Their world was his world.
But words on paper were not enough for him. He wanted to make a confession of faith in stone.
So on the shore of Lake Zurich, at a place called Bollingen, he built himself a tower with his own hands.
He had no electricity, no running water. He pumped water from the well, chopped his own wood, lit the oil lamps at night, and cooked his own food.
These simple acts make a man simple, he said. And how difficult it is to be simple!
For him, the tower was a kind of womb, a place where he could become what he was, what he is, and what he would be. He felt reborn in stone.
And he never forgot the stone from his childhood garden.
# The Edge of the World
> "Death is psychologically as important as birth, and, like it, is an integral part of life." Carl Jung, The Soul and Death.
In nineteen forty-four, Jung slipped on the ice, broke his foot, and then suffered a heart attack. For days, he hung between life and death.
And on that edge he had the most powerful visions of his life.
He found himself high above the earth. Far below, he saw the globe, bathed in a glorious blue light. He saw the deserts of Arabia, the snowy Himalayas, the dark sea.
Then he turned, and floating in space next to him was a huge block of dark stone, hollowed into a temple. At its entrance, an Indian sage sat silently, waiting for him.
As he walked toward the entrance, everything he had wished for, everything he had aimed for, the whole show of earthly life, was stripped away from him. It was painful.
And yet something remained. He was made of his own history. Everything he had lived, he now carried with him. This is what I am, he thought.
And he knew that inside the temple, he would finally understand where he came from, why he had been born, and where his life was flowing.
But before he could enter, his own doctor appeared, floating up from the earth, with a message: he had no right to leave yet. He had to go back.
And the vision ended.
Jung was bitterly disappointed. For weeks, the real world looked to him like a painted curtain with black holes in it. Life felt like a prison of little boxes, where each person sat alone in his own box.
But at night, around midnight, the visions returned. He felt as though he were floating in the womb of the universe, in an infinite void, filled with a happiness he could not describe.
# Kindle a Light
> "Who looks outside, dreams. Who looks inside, awakes." Carl Jung, in a letter.
In old age, Jung was asked again and again what happens after death. He did not pretend to know.
But he was sure of one thing. Whatever lies beyond, life on earth matters enormously. Because only here, where the opposites clash, where we suffer and choose, can consciousness grow.
And that, he believed, is our task. Not to escape the darkness, but to carry a small light into it.
He said the decisive question for any human being is this: are you related to something infinite, or not?
If you feel, even here, even now, a link to the infinite, then your desires change. Your whole attitude changes.
And he did not mean anything grand. He meant doing your best, in the place you are in, with the means you have. Even something small, done with all your heart, moves the whole of humanity forward.
The question is not whether your life was big. The question is whether you answered your own calling.
Because if you don't, Jung warned, the problem doesn't disappear. It goes into a neurosis. Or it passes down to your children, who must carry the weight of your unlived life on top of their own.
So he chose to suffer, if he had to, for things that were truly his own.
Near the end, he looked back and said he was satisfied. Nothing had gone as he expected. Everything had happened because he was who he was.
The older he got, the less certain he felt about himself, and the more he felt a kinship with all things: plants, animals, clouds, day and night.
A few days before he died, he described one last dream.
He saw a great round stone in a high place, in the full sun. Carved into it were words telling him that this was a sign of the wholeness he had achieved.
Then he saw trees, their roots reaching up from the ground and wrapping around him, threaded with gold.
Those around him said he smiled often, and was happy.
Carl Gustav Jung died on the sixth of June, nineteen sixty-one, at his home by the lake.
A short while later, a great storm broke over the house. Lightning struck his favourite tree in the garden.
Above the door of that house he had carved an old Latin saying, and today it is carved on his grave too. Vocatus atque non vocatus, deus aderit. Called or not called, God will be present.
The flower has passed. The root remains.

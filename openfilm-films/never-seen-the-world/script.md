## Opening: The Stone

On a summer morning in 1763, two men walked out of a church in the English town of Harwich, arguing about whether the world exists.

One was James Boswell, a young Scottish lawyer. The other was Samuel Johnson, the most feared talker in England. The subject was a bishop named George Berkeley, who had made a claim so strange that nobody knew how to answer it. Matter, Berkeley said, does not exist on its own. A thing is real only as far as some mind perceives it. To be is to be perceived.

Boswell admitted he could not refute it. Johnson did not bother with words. He walked up to a large stone, and kicked it, so hard that his body bounced back. "I refute it thus."

For two and a half centuries, that kick has been the answer of every practical man to every dreamer. The stone is there. Believe what you like. It will still break your toe.

It is a good answer. It is also wrong, or at least, it answers a question nobody asked. Because look at what Johnson actually had in that moment. A feeling of hardness. A jolt in the leg. A flash of pain. A grey shape in his eye. Every one of those things happened inside Samuel Johnson. He never touched the stone. He touched his own experience of it.

And so have you. You have never, not once, stepped outside your own mind to check what is out there. This film is about what follows from that.

## I. The Colour of Nothing

Start with something simple. The sky is blue. Except that it isn't.

Isaac Newton, the man who split sunlight with a prism, was careful about this. "For the Rays, to speak properly, are not coloured," he wrote. "In them there is nothing else than a certain Power and Disposition to stir up a Sensation of this or that Colour." *(Newton, Opticks, 1704)*

Read that again slowly. Light has no colour. Light has a wavelength. Blue is what a mind does with it.

Galileo had said the same a lifetime earlier. Tastes, smells and colours, he argued, are only names, as far as the object itself is concerned. They live in the creature that senses them. Take away the creature, and they are wiped out.

So picture the world before there were any eyes. No blue sky. No green forest. No sound of the sea, only pressure moving through air with nobody for it to become a sound in. No sweetness in the fruit. No warmth in the sun, only faster and slower particles.

That silent, colourless, tasteless place is the world of physics. And nobody has ever lived there. The world you live in, the one with sunsets and music and the smell of rain, is built somewhere else. It is built in you.

Even now your mind is editing. Each of your eyes has a blind spot, a hole in its vision where the nerve leaves the eye. You have never noticed it, because your brain quietly paints over the gap with its best guess. You are not looking through a window. You are looking at a painting, and the painter is still at work.

## II. The Word

In the spring of 1887, in a small town in Alabama, a girl of six stood by a water pump. She had been blind and deaf since she was a baby. She lived, she later said, in a kind of fog, with no words and no way to hold a thought.

Her teacher, Anne Sullivan, held one of the girl's hands under the running water. Into the other hand she spelled a word with her fingers, again and again. W, a, t, e, r.

And something happened that Helen Keller could describe for the rest of her life. "I knew then that 'w-a-t-e-r' meant the wonderful cool something that was flowing over my hand. That living word awakened my soul, gave it light, hope, joy, set it free!" *(Helen Keller, The Story of My Life, 1903)*

She ran through the garden touching everything, demanding its name. "Everything had a name," she wrote, "and each name gave birth to a new thought."

Notice what did not change that day. The water was the same water. The garden was the same garden. Not one atom moved. And yet by evening, Helen Keller was living in a different world, one with things in it.

We imagine that the world comes to us already cut into pieces. Here a rock, there a river, there a tree. But the cutting is ours. Nature does not draw a line where the hill ends and the valley begins. A mind draws it, gives it a name, and then forgets it ever held the pen.

## III. The Agreement

Now put many minds together, and something stranger happens. They start to build things out of nothing but agreement.

Take a banknote out of your pocket. It is a piece of printed paper. You cannot eat it or wear it or burn it for much warmth. It will buy your dinner for one reason only: because you believe it will, and so does the man who takes it, and so does everyone he will hand it to.

A border is a line that no bird has ever seen. A law is a sentence. A company has no body. A nation is a story told by millions of people at once. None of these can be weighed or photographed. And men will work their whole lives for them, and die for them.

In 1928, the sociologist William Thomas put it into one line. "If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences." *(W. I. Thomas and D. S. Thomas, The Child in America, 1928)*

Twenty years later, Robert Merton gave the classic case. Picture a sound, healthy bank. One morning a rumour starts that it is about to fail. The rumour is false. But people believe it, so they queue up to pull their money out. And a bank that everyone leaves at once does fail. The belief was untrue when it began. It made itself true.

Merton called it the self-fulfilling prophecy. It works on banks. It works on marriages, on teams, on countries. It works on a man who decides, at twenty, what kind of man he is.

So how much of what you call the real world is of this kind? Not stone. Agreement, held so long that it has gone hard.

## IV. The Fourth Man

In May of 1916, three men crossed the island of South Georgia on foot. Their ship had been crushed by the Antarctic ice. They had sailed eight hundred miles of the worst ocean on earth in a lifeboat. Now they had to cross mountains nobody had ever mapped, with a length of rope and a carpenter's tool for an ice axe, to reach a whaling station on the far side. Twenty-two men left behind would die if they failed.

They walked for thirty-six hours without sleep. They made it.

Years later, their leader, Ernest Shackleton, wrote down something he had hesitated to tell. "I know that during that long and racking march of thirty-six hours over the unnamed mountains and glaciers of South Georgia it seemed to me often that we were four, not three." *(Ernest Shackleton, South, 1919)*

He had said nothing at the time. Then one of the others confessed that he had felt it too. A fourth, walking beside them.

You can explain this any way you like. Exhaustion. Cold. A brain at the edge of its strength. All of that is probably true. But notice the shape of what happened. At the limit, the mind did not report less of the world. It supplied more. It put a companion on the ice, and the three men walked better for it.

The psychologist William James spent years collecting moments like this, and reached a careful conclusion. "Our normal waking consciousness," he wrote, "is but one special type of consciousness, whilst all about it, parted from it by the filmiest of screens, there lie potential forms of consciousness entirely different." *(William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience, 1902)*

The ordinary world is one setting of the dial. It is the useful setting, the one that finds food and avoids cliffs. It is not the only one, and nobody has shown that it is the truest.

## V. What the Physicists Said

You might expect the men who study matter to have no patience for any of this. Some of the greatest of them surprised everybody.

Max Planck founded quantum theory. He spent his life measuring the hardest facts there are. In 1931, a newspaper asked him whether consciousness could be explained by matter. His answer was flat. "I regard consciousness as fundamental. I regard matter as derivative from consciousness. We cannot get behind consciousness." *(Max Planck, interview in The Observer, 25 January 1931)*

Erwin Schrödinger, who wrote the central equation of that theory, went further. He had read the old Indian texts, and he thought they had seen something the West had missed. Consciousness, he wrote, "is a singular of which the plural is unknown." *(Erwin Schrödinger, What Is Life?, 1944)*

Be careful here. These were opinions, not experiments. Physics has not proved that mind makes the world, and plenty of physicists think Planck was wrong. Anyone who tells you that science has settled this, in either direction, is selling something.

But the puzzle that moved them is real, and it has not gone away. Science can tell you which cells fire when you see red. It cannot tell you why that firing feels like anything at all. Why there is somebody home. Every explanation we have starts from the outside and works in, and the inside is the one thing we are sure of.

Planck simply turned the map around. Perhaps the mind is not a late accident in a dead universe. Perhaps it was there at the bottom all along.

## VI. The Dreamer

If that is so, then who exactly is doing the perceiving?

David Hume went looking. He turned his attention inward to find his own self, the solid "I" that owns all the thoughts. He came back empty-handed. "I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception." *(David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, 1739)*

A feeling, a memory, a wish, an itch. Never the one who has them. The self, Hume decided, is a bundle of perceptions, moving very fast.

The Buddha had said something close to it two thousand years before. "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts." *(The Dhammapada, tr. Max Müller)*

And in China, a philosopher named Zhuangzi woke one afternoon from a dream of being a butterfly, fluttering about, happy, with no idea of being a man. Then he lay there, unsure. "Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly dreaming I am a man." *(Zhuangzi, tr. Herbert Giles)*

It sounds like a joke. Try to answer it. Every test you could run to prove you are awake, you could also dream.

The oldest Indian texts take the last step. In one of them, a father tells his son to drop a lump of salt into a bowl of water, and to bring it back the next morning. The salt is gone. Taste the water from the top, the father says. Salt. From the middle. Salt. From the bottom. Salt. You cannot see it, and it is everywhere. So it is with what you are. That fine essence runs through all things. "That art thou." *(Chandogya Upanishad)*

Not a small self looking out at a large world. One awareness, looking at itself through a great many eyes.

## VII. Dust and Centre

Here is the part that is hard to hold in one head.

Measured from outside, you are almost nothing. A short life on a small planet, around one star among more stars than there are grains of sand on every beach. Blaise Pascal felt that cold as sharply as anyone ever has. And then he noticed the other half. "By space the universe encompasses and swallows me up like an atom; by thought I comprehend the world." *(Pascal, Pensées, tr. W. F. Trotter)*

The universe contains you. And the only universe you will ever meet is contained in you. Both are true at once. A speck, and the centre.

Many people who reach this point describe a strange loneliness. Not the loneliness of having no friends. The dizzying sense that everything they have ever known, every face and every star, arrived as their own experience.

Stay with it a little longer, and it turns over. If there is no hard wall between you and the world, you were never locked out of it. Nothing is strange to you. You are not a visitor here.

## VIII. The Chinks of the Cavern

So what do you do with this on a Monday morning?

Not what the kick was afraid of. Nobody is asking you to walk through walls. The stone will still hurt your foot, and the bill will still come due. Habits of mind that are billions of years old do not vanish because you read a book.

But the heaviest things in your life are not made of stone. They are made of meaning, and meaning is yours.

The job you lost. The woman who left. The year that went wrong. The event happened. What it is, a disaster, a lesson, a release, a beginning, was not in the event. You put it there. John Milton gave the line to a fallen angel, standing in the ruins of everything he had: "The mind is its own place, and in itself can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven." *(Milton, Paradise Lost, Book I)*

It begins smaller than that. It begins with where you look. "My experience," William James wrote, "is what I agree to attend to." *(William James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890)* A man who looks all day for insults will find a world full of enemies. Another man, on the same street, finds something else. They are not seeing one world differently. In every way that matters to them, they live in different worlds.

William Blake thought we had all shrunk ours without noticing. "If the doors of perception were cleansed, every thing would appear to man as it is, infinite. For man has closed himself up, till he sees all things through narrow chinks of his cavern." *(William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell)*

The teachers who spoke of another world, a higher one, a world to come, may have meant something nearer than we assume. Not another place. This one, with the doors cleaned.

## Closing: The Stone Again

Go back to Harwich. The stone is still lying by the road. Johnson's foot is still swinging toward it.

He was right that something is there. He was wrong about what he proved. He did not kick his way out of his own mind. Nobody ever has. The hardness, the weight, the grey, the pain: all of it came to him the only way anything comes to anyone.

Blake looked at the same kind of stone and saw the rest of it. "To see a World in a Grain of Sand, and a Heaven in a Wild Flower. Hold Infinity in the palm of your hand, and Eternity in an hour." *(William Blake, Auguries of Innocence)*

You have never seen the world. You have only ever seen what a mind makes of it. That is not a loss. It means the world was never something that only happens to you.

You have been holding the pen the whole time.

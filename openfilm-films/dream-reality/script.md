# How to Make Your Dream a Reality

Written for this video from the user's reference ("How to Make Any Goal Become Reality"): the same storytelling (a quote,
a hook from history, two traps, one central story, then the method in steps, and a closing that ties every thread together),
in new words and with new stories. Sources: Thoreau, Walden (1854); Seneca, Letters 71; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 10.16;
the Wright brothers and Samuel Langley, 1899-1903; Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), implementation intentions.

## Opening
If you have built castles in the air, your work need not be lost. That is where they should be. Now put the foundations under them.
Henry David Thoreau wrote that in a cabin he built with his own hands, beside a pond in Massachusetts.
He was not against dreaming. He spent two years alone in the woods, chasing a dream most people thought was foolish.
But he understood something most dreamers never do.
A dream is only the roof. Nobody lives in a roof.
Most of us have a castle in the air. The book we will write. The body we will build. The life we will live once things finally calm down.
We visit it every night. We furnish it in detail. And every morning, we wake up in exactly the same place.
So this video is about one question.
How does a dream leave your head and enter the world?

## I. The castle and the ground
Start with the strangest race of the twentieth century.
In 1903, two teams were trying to build the first machine that could fly a man through the air.
The first was led by Samuel Langley, the head of the Smithsonian Institution, one of the most respected scientists in America.
He had fifty thousand dollars from the War Department, a staff of engineers, and the attention of the entire press.
The second team was two brothers from Ohio who repaired bicycles for a living.
No degrees. No funding. Wilbur and Orville Wright paid for everything out of their own shop.
On paper, it was not a race at all.

## II. The two traps
Langley's machine was called the Aerodrome. It was launched from a catapult on top of a houseboat in the Potomac River.
In October 1903, it left the catapult and dropped straight into the water.
In December, they tried again. It broke apart in the air and fell into the river a second time.
A newspaper wrote that a true flying machine might take one million to ten million years.
Langley had made the first mistake a dreamer can make. He went straight for the grand moment.
He tested the whole vision at once, in public, and when it failed, there was nothing to learn from but wreckage.
The second trap is quieter. It belongs to the man who never launches at all.
He reads, plans and prepares, waiting for the perfect moment and the perfect conditions.
Marcus Aurelius wrote a line for him. Waste no more time arguing about what a good man should be. Be one.
The first man leaps too soon. The second never leaps.
Both have mistaken the picture in their head for the work in the world.

## III. Foundations
The Wrights did something different. They started with the problem that had killed the men before them.
In 1896, the great German glider pioneer Otto Lilienthal fell from the sky and died.
The brothers studied his death and decided that lift was not the real problem. Control was.
So before they ever built an engine, they built gliders, and flew them, again and again, for three summers.
When their gliders kept disappointing them, they did not quit, and they did not trust the published tables.
They built a small wind tunnel in their shop and tested around two hundred different wing shapes, one at a time.
They even wrote to the Weather Bureau to find the windiest, emptiest beach in America. It was Kitty Hawk, North Carolina.
On the 17th of December 1903, just nine days after Langley's second crash, Orville Wright lay on the lower wing and flew.
Twelve seconds. One hundred and twenty feet. The first powered flight in history.
The whole project had cost them about a thousand dollars.
They did not have the bigger dream. They had the better foundations.

## IV. The method
So what are the foundations under a castle in the air?
First, name the dream precisely. Seneca wrote that if a man does not know to which port he is sailing, no wind is favourable.
Not "be successful", but the exact thing, the exact number, the exact date.
Second, find the thing that will kill it. Not the obstacles in the world. The obstacle in you.
Your impatience. Your fear of looking foolish. The habit of stopping when it gets boring.
Third, decide in advance what you will do when that obstacle appears. If this happens, then I will do that.
Across nearly a hundred studies, people who made plans in exactly that form were far more likely to reach their goals.
Fourth, build the glider before the aeroplane. Make the smallest version of your dream that can be tested this week.
Every small failure is a wind tunnel. Every big failure is the Potomac.
And finally, show up every day. The Wrights did not fly in one heroic leap. They flew after three summers of patient, unglamorous work.

## Close
Langley had the money, the fame and the vision. The brothers from Ohio had a method.
And that is the difference between a castle in the air and a house you can live in.
Keep your dream. It is where you are meant to live.
But tonight, before you sleep, put one stone underneath it.
The world will never answer what you imagine. It only answers what you build.

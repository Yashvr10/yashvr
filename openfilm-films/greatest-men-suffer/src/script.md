# Why the Greatest Men Must Suffer (script draft; every quotation verbatim from the Gutenberg edition named in README)

0 hook
s01 Every age has its comfortable men. They are safe, well fed, and forgotten. The names we remember belong to a different kind of man. Look closely at almost any of them, and you will find a wound.
s02 In eighteen eighty-six, Friedrich Nietzsche asked a question that still unsettles us. The discipline of suffering, of great suffering, he wrote, know ye not that it is only this discipline that has produced all the elevations of humanity hitherto?
s03 Is he right? Is suffering the price of greatness, or just its excuse? To answer that, we have to go back two thousand years, to the men who thought about it hardest.

I. Fire Tries Gold
s04 Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome, and the tutor of the emperor Nero. Yet he wrote an entire essay on one question: why, if the gods are good, do misfortunes fall on good men?
s05 His answer was blunt. God does not pet the good man, he wrote. He tries him, hardens him. Fire tries gold, misfortune tries brave men.
s06 For Seneca, hardship was not a punishment. It was a test, and the only way a man could ever discover what he was made of. It does not matter what you bear, he wrote, but how you bear it.

II. The Obstacle Is the Road
s07 A century later, the emperor Marcus Aurelius spent much of his reign at war on the frontier, while plague swept through the empire. At night, he wrote notes to himself.
s08 The mind, he wrote, converts and changes every hindrance to its activity into an aid. And so that which is a hindrance is made a furtherance to an act, and that which is an obstacle on the road helps us on this road.
s09 The obstacle was not in the way. The obstacle was the way.

III. Not Things, But Views
s10 The man who taught this most clearly had suffered more than either of them. Epictetus was born the son of a slave woman, and was himself a slave, in the household of one of Nero's freedmen.
s11 Men are disturbed, he taught, not by things, but by the views which they take of things.
s12 He could not choose his chains. He could only choose what he made of them. And a slave became one of the most influential teachers in the history of philosophy.

IV. The Deaf Composer
s13 In October eighteen hundred and two, a young composer wrote a letter to his brothers that he never sent. His name was Ludwig van Beethoven, and he was going deaf.
s14 He described the humiliation of standing beside someone who heard a flute in the distance, while he heard nothing. Such things, he wrote, brought me to the verge of desperation, and well nigh caused me to put an end to my life.
s15 Then he wrote the line that saved him. Art! Art alone deterred me. Ah! How could I possibly quit the world, before bringing forth all that I felt it was my vocation to produce?
s16 He chose patience as his guide, and kept working. Some of the greatest music ever written came after that letter, much of it composed by a man who could no longer hear it.

V. The Prisoner
s17 In fifteen seventy-one, a young Spanish soldier fought at the naval battle of Lepanto. He took three gunshot wounds, and lost the use of his left hand.
s18 On his way home, his ship was taken by pirates, and he spent five years as a captive in Algiers. Later, back in Spain, he was thrown into prison over a debt.
s19 His name was Miguel de Cervantes. And most scholars believe he began writing Don Quixote in a prison cell. It was published in sixteen oh five, and it is still read all over the world.

VI. The Blind Poet
s20 John Milton spent his early life preparing to write a great epic. By the time he was ready, he was completely blind.
s21 When I consider how my light is spent, he wrote, ere half my days, in this dark world and wide. He could have stopped there.
s22 Instead, he dictated Paradise Lost, line by line. So much the rather thou, Celestial light, shine inward, he wrote, that I may see and tell of things invisible to mortal sight.

VII. What Suffering Is For
s23 None of these men chose their suffering. Seneca did not ask for exile, nor Beethoven for silence, nor Milton for darkness. And suffering does not make everyone great. It breaks many people.
s24 What separates them is not the size of the wound, but what they did with it. Each of them turned pain into a work: a book, a symphony, a philosophy, a poem.
s25 Nietzsche put it in seven words. From the military school of life: that which does not kill me, makes me stronger.

VIII. How to Carry It
s26 So how do you carry your own weight? First, separate the thing from your view of it, as Epictetus did. Ask what part of this is the event, and what part is the story you are telling about it.
s27 Second, use the obstacle. Ask what this hardship makes possible, that comfort never would. What is it training in you?
s28 Third, give your pain a work. Beethoven did not wait to feel better before he composed. Put the weight into something that will outlast it.
s29 And fourth, choose patience. They also serve, Milton wrote, who only stand and wait.
s30 The greatest men did not suffer less than us. They suffered, and built anyway. That is the whole secret. And it is open to anyone.
